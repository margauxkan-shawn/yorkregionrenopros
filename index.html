Budgeting

How Much Does a Kitchen Remodel Cost in Toronto?

6 min read · Updated 2026

The honest answer is: it depends almost entirely on scope. A cosmetic refresh and a full gut renovation can differ by six figures on the exact same footprint. Here's a realistic breakdown of what each tier actually includes, so you can figure out which one fits your project before you start collecting quotes.

Cosmetic refresh — roughly $10,000 to $25,000

This tier keeps your existing layout and appliances. Think: refacing or repainting cabinets instead of replacing them, a new countertop, updated backsplash, new hardware and lighting, and a fresh coat of paint. It's the fastest option — often two to three weeks — and the one with the least disruption, since plumbing and electrical stay untouched.

Mid-range remodel — roughly $30,000 to $75,000

This is where most kitchen remodels land. It typically includes new cabinets (semi-custom or stock), new countertops, new appliances, updated flooring, and often some layout adjustments — moving an island, opening a wall partially, relocating a sink a few feet. Expect four to eight weeks, and expect permits if you're touching plumbing or load-bearing elements.

Full gut renovation — $75,000 and up

Everything down to the studs: full layout changes, moved plumbing and electrical, custom cabinetry, premium countertops and appliances, and often structural work like removing a wall between kitchen and living room. Eight to sixteen weeks is typical, and this tier is where permit and inspection timelines start to meaningfully affect your schedule.

Rule of thumb: moving plumbing or gas lines is one of the biggest cost multipliers in any kitchen project. If your budget is tight, keeping the sink, stove, and dishwasher in roughly their current locations is the single easiest way to control cost.

What actually drives the price

Cabinetry

Usually the single largest line item — often 30–40% of the total budget. Stock cabinets are the budget option, semi-custom sits in the middle, and fully custom cabinetry can double or triple that portion of the cost.

Countertops

Laminate is the low-cost option; quartz and granite sit in the middle to upper range; natural stone slabs like marble are the premium tier, both in material cost and installation complexity.

Layout changes

Moving a wall, relocating plumbing, or changing electrical panel capacity adds cost fast — not just materials, but permits, inspections, and often a structural engineer's sign-off if a wall is load-bearing.

Permits

In Toronto, permits are typically required for any project involving plumbing relocation, electrical work beyond like-for-like fixture swaps, or structural changes. Skipping a required permit can create real problems later — at resale, with insurance claims, or with the city directly.

How to keep costs under control

  • Keep plumbing and gas fixtures in their existing locations where possible
  • Choose semi-custom over fully custom cabinetry — it closes most of the visual gap at a fraction of the cost
  • Get at least two quotes at the same scope level, so you're comparing apples to apples
  • Budget an extra 10–15% contingency — almost every remodel uncovers something once walls come open

Hiring

10 Questions to Ask Before You Hire a Contractor

7 min read · Updated 2026

Most renovation horror stories trace back to skipping a handful of basic questions before signing anything. None of these require special expertise to ask — just a willingness to slow down before you hand over a deposit.

1. Are you insured, and can I see proof?

A legitimate contractor carries general liability insurance and should be able to provide a certificate on request — not just tell you they have it. This protects you if something goes wrong on your property during the project.

2. Are you registered with WSIB?

In Ontario, contractors and their workers should be covered by the Workplace Safety and Insurance Board. Without it, you could be held liable if someone is injured working on your property. You can verify WSIB clearance status directly through WSIB's online lookup.

3. Can I see references from recent, similar projects?

Ask specifically for projects similar in scope to yours, completed in the last year, and actually call them. A contractor confident in their work will have no hesitation providing this.

4. Will you pull the necessary permits?

If your project needs permits — most plumbing, electrical, or structural work does — a legitimate contractor pulls them and builds the timeline into your schedule. Be wary of anyone who suggests skipping permits to save time or money.

5. What's the payment schedule?

A reasonable schedule is tied to project milestones, not front-loaded. Be cautious of any contractor asking for more than 10–20% upfront, or asking for full payment before work is substantially complete.

6. Is the estimate itemized?

A detailed, line-item quote (materials, labour, permits, contingency) is far easier to compare across contractors than a single lump-sum number — and makes it much harder for costs to balloon without explanation partway through.

7. Who will actually be doing the work?

Ask directly whether subcontractors will be used, and if so, who's responsible for their insurance and quality of work. You want a clear answer, not a vague one.

8. What happens if we hit a delay or a surprise?

Almost every renovation uncovers something unexpected once walls are open. Ask how change orders are handled — in writing, with your sign-off, before work proceeds — rather than finding out after the fact.

9. What's the warranty on the work?

Reputable contractors typically offer at least a one-year workmanship warranty, separate from manufacturer warranties on materials and appliances. Get this in writing.

10. Can I get everything in a written contract?

Verbal agreements are unenforceable if something goes wrong. A proper contract should include scope of work, materials, timeline, payment schedule, and warranty terms — all before a single tool touches your home.

Red flag to know: a contractor who pressures you to decide immediately, asks for payment in cash only, or can't produce insurance and WSIB documentation on request is worth walking away from — regardless of how good the quote looks.

Aging-in-Place Renovation Checklist

5 min read · Updated 2026

Aging-in-place renovations aren't just for seniors — they matter for anyone planning to stay in a home long-term, recovering from an injury, or supporting a family member with mobility needs. The goal is the same either way: reduce fall risk, keep daily tasks manageable, and avoid a future move that's harder than it needs to be.

Bathroom

  • Walk-in or curbless shower — removes the trip hazard of stepping over a tub edge
  • Grab bars — installed into wall studs (not just drywall) near the toilet and shower
  • Comfort-height toilet — a couple of inches taller than standard, easier on knees and hips
  • Non-slip flooring — textured tile or slip-resistant vinyl instead of polished stone
  • Handheld showerhead — allows showering seated if needed

Doorways and hallways

  • Widen doorways to at least 32–36 inches if a walker or wheelchair may ever be needed
  • Remove or bevel thresholds between rooms to reduce trip risk
  • Ensure hallways are wide enough for two people or mobility equipment to pass

Living spaces

  • Consider converting a main-floor room into a bedroom if stairs are a long-term concern
  • Lever-style door handles instead of knobs — easier to operate with limited grip strength
  • Improved lighting throughout, especially stairwells and hallways — brighter and more even, not just more fixtures

Stairs

  • Sturdy handrails on both sides, not just one
  • Non-slip stair treads
  • A stairlift is worth evaluating if a full main-floor conversion isn't practical

Kitchen

  • Lower or adjustable-height counters and cabinets in at least one work zone
  • Pull-out shelves instead of deep fixed cabinets — easier to reach without bending or climbing
  • Front-facing controls on the stove, reducing the need to reach over hot burners
Planning tip: if you're not ready for a full renovation, prioritize the bathroom first. It's the room where falls are most common, and even a partial update — grab bars, a curbless shower pan, non-slip flooring — meaningfully reduces risk.

A note on basement suites

For multigenerational households, finishing a basement into a self-contained suite is a common aging-in-place move — it keeps family close while preserving independence and privacy. In Toronto, this typically means a separate entrance, proper egress windows, and meeting current fire-separation code, so it's worth involving a contractor familiar with legal basement apartment requirements specifically.

When to start planning

The honest advice: earlier than feels necessary. Aging-in-place renovations are far easier — and cheaper — to plan proactively than to retrofit after a fall or a sudden mobility change forces the issue.
