Aging-in-Place Renovation Checklist

5 min read · Updated 2026

Aging-in-place renovations aren't just for seniors — they matter for anyone planning to stay in a home long-term, recovering from an injury, or supporting a family member with mobility needs. The goal is the same either way: reduce fall risk, keep daily tasks manageable, and avoid a future move that's harder than it needs to be.

Bathroom

  • Walk-in or curbless shower — removes the trip hazard of stepping over a tub edge
  • Grab bars — installed into wall studs (not just drywall) near the toilet and shower
  • Comfort-height toilet — a couple of inches taller than standard, easier on knees and hips
  • Non-slip flooring — textured tile or slip-resistant vinyl instead of polished stone
  • Handheld showerhead — allows showering seated if needed

Doorways and hallways

  • Widen doorways to at least 32–36 inches if a walker or wheelchair may ever be needed
  • Remove or bevel thresholds between rooms to reduce trip risk
  • Ensure hallways are wide enough for two people or mobility equipment to pass

Living spaces

  • Consider converting a main-floor room into a bedroom if stairs are a long-term concern
  • Lever-style door handles instead of knobs — easier to operate with limited grip strength
  • Improved lighting throughout, especially stairwells and hallways — brighter and more even, not just more fixtures

Stairs

  • Sturdy handrails on both sides, not just one
  • Non-slip stair treads
  • A stairlift is worth evaluating if a full main-floor conversion isn't practical

Kitchen

  • Lower or adjustable-height counters and cabinets in at least one work zone
  • Pull-out shelves instead of deep fixed cabinets — easier to reach without bending or climbing
  • Front-facing controls on the stove, reducing the need to reach over hot burners
Planning tip: if you're not ready for a full renovation, prioritize the bathroom first. It's the room where falls are most common, and even a partial update — grab bars, a curbless shower pan, non-slip flooring — meaningfully reduces risk.

A note on basement suites

For multigenerational households, finishing a basement into a self-contained suite is a common aging-in-place move — it keeps family close while preserving independence and privacy. In Toronto, this typically means a separate entrance, proper egress windows, and meeting current fire-separation code, so it's worth involving a contractor familiar with legal basement apartment requirements specifically.

When to start planning

The honest advice: earlier than feels necessary. Aging-in-place renovations are far easier — and cheaper — to plan proactively than to retrofit after a fall or a sudden mobility change forces the issue.
